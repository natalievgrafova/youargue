Argument mining on YouTube comments

YouArgue Pipeline

How the system collects videos, proposes topics for videos nobody annotated, and scores relevance and stance — with what each model is worth, what is finished, what is waiting, and the questions still open.

How it works

Two paths run through the same app. The gold path serves human annotations for the videos annotated by hand. The model path serves predictions for any video, including ones no annotator has seen. Both answer the same endpoint; a source parameter chooses between them, and gold is the default, so the app behaves exactly as before when no predictions exist.

No model runs while a page loads. Predictions are computed ahead of time on a GPU and shipped as a file, which is why the app answers in milliseconds and needs no accelerator of its own.

Where the topics come from

Relevance and stance are both asked about a topic. An annotated video has a gold topic list; a new video has none, and free generation is weak — the best target-generation arm scores 0.27 F1. So the problem is inverted: keep a fixed pool of candidate issues, rank it against the video, and extract the video-specific ones from the comments themselves.

Stance is the strong layer, and the confidence signal is real enough to show a certainty badge — the Dutch figure of 0.851 overturns an earlier measurement of 0.53 taken from a prompted ensemble rather than a fine-tuned adapter.

Skewed topics are expected, not a defect. Some labels draw a very one-sided split — terrorism 15 pro / 119 con, shooting 7 / 93. That is what the audience thinks, and the small minority side is real: people do argue for revenge, or against press freedom. Rare, but they occur, and they are worth capturing rather than filtering out.
Topic relevance is the weak layer. The model flags 61 % of English pairs relevant where truth is 27 %, and assigns 5.05 topics per comment where gold has 2.23. Recall is fine at 0.88; it over-fires when positives are rare. No threshold could be set because prompt_eval.py wrote a constant 0.5 for every binary probability — fixed, and the re-runs are queued.

Every human test row comes from the same 100 comments per language; the topic task scores each comment against the whole inventory, the stance task only the pairs a human judged relevant with a side. Stance excludes none, matching how English and Dutch were trained.

All 300 test comments were checked by hand, not sampled. Two models annotated independently — Claude and GPT-5.5 — every pair where at least one said "relevant" was checked by a human (430 pairs), and the remaining 74 comments, where both models found nothing, were curated too. Train and test are disjoint by YouTube comment_id.

The human check produced findings of its own. Model agreement is not a proxy for correctness: 11 % of the pairs both annotators agreed on were overturned, including five outright polarity reversals, three of them irony. And disagreement predicts ambiguity — the human marked none on 9 % of agreed pairs but 43 % of relevance disagreements and 59 % of stance disagreements.

Yes. That justified annotating the remaining 900 comments per language with GPT-5.5 — 2,700 comments, 49,500 silver pairs, for about $14 — and training on them with the 300 human-verified comments held out. Two asymmetries shaped the run: GPT is the stronger annotator where they differ (κ 0.955 against 0.706 in Russian, because Claude systematically under-tags), and German is the weakest language, where both annotators agree with each other while differing from the human.

The splits hold out whole threads, from two different videos. Replies carry their root's text as context, so splitting comments at random would put the same root on both sides; and the corpus is exactly ten threads of a hundred per language, so a percentage split would make validation a single conversation.

Open questions

Places where the current answer is unsatisfying and someone thinking freshly could help.

1. Multilingual models, and whether English adapters should transfer at all

DE/FR/RU are currently served by English adapters applied cross-lingually. Measured on authentic comments, that transfer is weak — and stranger, it works best with no context at all:

Do not confuse these with the older DE/FR/RU numbers. An earlier round (outputs_translated/, e.g. de topic 0.805, de stance 0.656) was measured on the English test set machine-translated into each language — 485 topic and 367 stance rows, identical in every language, because it is the same English data. Those figures describe translationese, not real target-language comments, and are consistently higher than the authentic ones above.

A German or Russian title appears to be off-distribution noise between the topic and the comment, since the adapter was fine-tuned on English ones. Open: does a genuinely multilingual base (Aya, EuroLLM, XLM-R) beat an English adapter here, and is per-language fine-tuning worth it at 900 silver comments per language? Debate-trained German and Russian models already exist and were scored on the new test sets; the comparison is available.

2. The topic inventory for an unseen video

Candidates come from Wikipedia’s controversial-issues list plus YAKE keyphrases from the comments. Ranked against the comment thread rather than the title, they cover roughly 70–80 % of annotated comment mass. What fails is selection: a flood video returns Flood, The Genesis Flood, God, Creationism and Biblical literalism as five topics for one argument, and Judas Iscariot alongside them.

Four filters were tried and only one half-works: embedding merging fails (it wanted to merge the left with the right, similarity 0.89); diversity re-ranking fails (it cut climate change and kept Judas Iscariot); a Wikipedia category cap fails (no two members of the religion cluster share a single leaf category); lexical containment works on Flood/Genesis Flood but deletes Human rights in China in favour of bare China. Open: is a learned final pass over the relevance model’s aggregate evidence the right answer, and can it be trained on 25 videos without simply memorising them?

3. Evaluation with very few videos

Every split here holds out a handful of videos, and two hold out one. Seeds do not help — they control the wrong variance. Open: how should a corpus of 4–8 videos per language be split so a number means something, and is leave-one-video-out with a reported spread the honest minimum?
